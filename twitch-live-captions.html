Twitch Live Captions — AI Real-Time Subtitles for Streamers

Twitch doesn't offer native live captions for viewers, but millions of people watch streams in environments where sound isn't ideal — loud places, late nights, hearing difficulties, or a different language. Twitch live captions from Live Subtitles add a real-time AI subtitle layer to any stream on your Windows PC, no Twitch Extension needed.

How to enable live captions on Twitch in 3 steps

  1. Download Live Subtitles from the Microsoft Store and launch the app.
  2. Select "System Audio" as the audio source — captures the stream's audio directly from your speakers.
  3. Open any Twitch stream — captions appear in a floating overlay that stays on top of the browser or Twitch app.

Why Twitch viewers need live captions

Twitch streams are live — there's no pause, no transcript, and no rewind for most viewers. For a growing international audience, following a fast-talking streamer with a regional accent can be exhausting. AI live captions convert the streamer's voice to text in under a second, making every word readable.

  • Deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers get full access to any Twitch stream.
  • Non-native speakers follow language-heavy streams without falling behind.
  • Viewers in quiet environments (libraries, offices, late night) use captions instead of audio.
  • Language learners watch foreign-language streamers with real-time captions or translation.

Translate Twitch streams into your language

Live Subtitles supports dual subtitle mode: the streamer's speech appears in the original language on the top line, and your chosen translation appears below — both update in real time. This is the closest thing to watching a Twitch stream dubbed into your language, but it works for any streamer, any language, instantly.

Supported language pairs include: English ↔ Spanish, English ↔ Japanese, English ↔ Chinese, English ↔ Korean, English ↔ Russian, English ↔ Portuguese, English ↔ French, English ↔ German, and 40+ more combinations.

Game Mode — captions during fullscreen gaming + streams

Many Twitch viewers watch streams while playing another game — picture-in-picture, second monitor, or background audio. Live Subtitles includes Game Mode (Ctrl+Shift+L) that locks the overlay in a click-through state, so captions stay visible over fullscreen games without interfering with inputs. Switch between watching a stream and playing without losing your captions.

For streamers: caption your own voice

Streamers can also use Live Subtitles for their own benefit during a session:

  • Read incoming chat from a language you don't speak — translate messages in real time.
  • Follow voice calls with raid guests or co-streamers in other languages.
  • Use microphone mode to caption your own speech as a personal prompter.

To be clear about the limit: this is a viewer-side overlay on your own screen. Your audience does not see it. For captions your viewers can read, you need a broadcaster-side tool — see the OBS subtitles guide and our comparison of every Twitch closed-caption option.

Watching streamers who don't speak your language

Twitch discovery is heavily regional — the front page you see is shaped by your language settings, and most viewers never leave it. But the interesting parts of a category are often somewhere else entirely: the Korean and Chinese scenes in competitive titles, the Japanese art and music streams, the Brazilian variety channels that run when your own timezone is asleep.

The barrier there is never the video, it is the commentary. Dual subtitle mode is aimed exactly at this: the streamer's speech stays on the top line in the original language, your language appears directly beneath it, and both update live. You keep the streamer's actual voice and delivery while reading what they mean — which is also why language learners use this setup deliberately rather than reluctantly.

It also handles the case native captions cannot touch at all: a stream where the host switches between two languages mid-sentence, which is normal on bilingual channels and completely defeats a single-language caption track.

Captions for VODs, clips and co-streams

Nothing about this approach is specific to live video. Because the app captions whatever audio is playing, the same overlay works on a Twitch VOD you are catching up on, on a clip compilation, or on a co-stream where two channels are running side by side in separate tabs.

That matters more than it sounds, because caption coverage on VODs is worse than on live streams. Extension overlays do not survive into the recording at all, so even a channel that captions its live broadcasts usually has entirely uncaptioned VODs and clips. A viewer-side overlay does not distinguish between them.

What Twitch gives you natively — and what it doesn't

Twitch is the largest live platform with no first-party automatic captions. Unlike YouTube Live, which can generate captions itself (in English only, on normal-latency streams), Twitch has no setting that produces captions for you. Everything that puts text on a Twitch stream is third-party, and all of it is installed by the streamer:

  • The OBS captions plugin feeds Twitch's native caption track. Viewers can toggle it, and it survives into the VOD — but it only works if the streamer runs OBS.
  • Caption extensions such as Stream Closed Captioner draw an overlay on the player. They work with any encoder, but the text is not part of the video, so VODs and clips are uncaptioned.
  • Browser-source services burn captions into the frame. Everyone sees them, nobody can turn them off, and they cost a monthly fee.

The practical consequence for a viewer is simple: on the overwhelming majority of channels, none of the above is running. You cannot install anything on someone else's stream, and waiting for thousands of individual streamers to each set up captioning is not a plan. That is the entire reason viewer-side captioning exists.

Latency and accuracy on stream audio

Captions appear roughly a second behind the speech. That delay is inherent to speech recognition — the system needs enough audio to commit to a word — and it does not slow the stream down. Live Subtitles reads audio that is already playing on your machine, so the video runs at normal speed with the text trailing slightly behind, the way broadcast captions work on television.

Accuracy on Twitch audio depends less on the app than on what the streamer's mix looks like. A host on a decent microphone in a quiet room transcribes cleanly. What degrades it is specific and predictable:

  • Loud game audio or music sitting close to voice level in the mix.
  • Several people talking over each other during a collab or a raid.
  • Heavy stream compression on a low-bitrate mobile broadcast.
  • Dense in-game jargon and proper nouns — champion names, map callouts, emote spellings — which every speech model handles worst.

Two adjustments help more than any setting: nudge the stream volume up relative to other system sounds, and set the source language explicitly instead of leaving auto-detection on when you already know what the streamer speaks.

Does the streamer or chat see anything?

No. The overlay is drawn on your own desktop by an app running locally — it is not injected into the Twitch player, it is not a browser extension, and it sends nothing to the channel. The streamer sees no indicator, chat sees no message, and nothing appears in your VOD or clips of the stream. From Twitch's perspective you are an ordinary viewer watching a stream with the sound on.

If captions don't appear

Nearly every "no captions" case comes down to one of four things:

  • The source is set to Microphone. Microphone mode captions you, not the stream. Switch it to System Audio.
  • The browser is playing to a different output device. If Twitch is going to a headset while the app listens to desktop speakers, there is no signal. Match them.
  • The tab is muted. Muting removes the audio entirely; lowering the volume is fine.
  • Exclusive fullscreen is covering the overlay. Press Ctrl+Shift+L for Game Mode, or move the overlay to a second monitor.
