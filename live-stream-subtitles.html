Live Stream Subtitles — Real-Time Captions for Any Stream

Recorded video is a solved problem. You open a YouTube upload or a Netflix episode, click the caption button, and the subtitle track is already there. Live stream subtitles are a different situation entirely: there is no finished transcript to attach, no time to correct mistakes, and on most platforms the decision about whether captions exist at all belongs to the streamer, not to you.

That is why so many people end up searching for "how to get subtitles on livestream" and find nothing useful. The answer is not a platform setting. It is a viewer-side caption layer that transcribes the audio playing on your own machine — which works on every platform at once, including the ones with no caption feature at all.

How to get subtitles on any live stream in 3 steps

  1. Install Live Subtitles from the Microsoft Store and launch it before you open the stream.
  2. Select "System Audio" as the source, so the app transcribes whatever is playing through your speakers.
  3. Open the stream on any site or app — subtitles appear in a floating overlay you can drag anywhere on screen.

There is nothing to configure on the streaming platform, no browser extension to install, and no cooperation needed from the person broadcasting. If you can hear it, it gets captioned.

Why live streams are the hardest content to caption

Three things make live audio genuinely difficult, and understanding them explains why platform-native captions are so patchy.

First, there is no second pass. Subtitles for a finished video can be generated slowly, reviewed, and corrected. A live caption has to commit to a word within a second of hearing it, with no knowledge of what comes next in the sentence.

Second, streaming audio is messy by nature. A single stream can mix a fast-talking host, game audio, music, alerts, and several people on a voice call with different microphones and accents. Broadcast captioning systems are tuned for one clear speaker in a studio.

Third, and most importantly for viewers: captions are the broadcaster's setting. Even where a platform supports live captions, they only appear if the streamer turned them on. From the audience side, that is a coin flip on every stream you open.

Multi-language streaming: watch a stream in a language you don't speak

This is the gap that native captions do not even attempt to fill. Streaming is global — a Korean StarCraft stream, a Brazilian variety channel, a Japanese art stream — but the caption tooling is not.

Live Subtitles handles this with dual subtitle mode: the speaker's words appear in the original language on the top line, and your chosen translation appears directly below, both updating in real time. You are not choosing between hearing the original and understanding it — you get both at once, which is also why this setup is popular with language learners.

Recognition and translation cover 50+ language pairs, including English ↔ Spanish, English ↔ Japanese, English ↔ Korean, English ↔ Chinese, English ↔ Portuguese, English ↔ Russian, English ↔ German, English ↔ French, and combinations that do not involve English at all.

Because the app captions system audio rather than hooking into a platform, the same setup works on a stream, a VOD, a Discord call, and a video in a browser tab — you do not reconfigure anything when you switch sites.

Viewer-side vs broadcaster-side captions — which one do you need?

These are two different jobs, and conflating them is the most common mistake in this category. Be clear about which one you are solving:

  • Viewer-side (this page). Subtitles appear on your screen, for your benefit, on any stream you open. Nobody else sees them. This is what Live Subtitles does.
  • Broadcaster-side. Captions are encoded into the stream so your audience sees them. This requires tooling in your streaming setup — the OBS captions plugin, a Twitch caption extension, or a browser-source service.

If you are a viewer, broadcaster-side tools are irrelevant to you — you cannot install anything on someone else's stream. If you are a streamer, you may well want both: a broadcaster-side tool for your audience, and a viewer-side overlay so you can follow a co-streamer, a raid guest, or a foreign-language game yourself. Our OBS subtitles guide covers where that line falls in a real streaming setup.

Watching a stream while gaming

A large share of stream viewing happens alongside something else — a stream on a second monitor while you play, or picture-in-picture on a single screen. Normal overlay windows break here: fullscreen games either hide them or steal every click.

Live Subtitles has a Game Mode (Ctrl+Shift+L) that locks the caption overlay in a click-through state so it stays readable above fullscreen content without intercepting mouse input. You can keep following a stream's commentary while playing, and reposition or move the overlay to a second display at any time. The Game Mode subtitles page covers the overlay settings in detail.

Latency and accuracy — what to expect

Subtitles appear roughly a second behind the speech. That gap is inherent to speech recognition: the system needs enough audio to decide what a word was. Crucially, it adds no delay to the stream itself — the app reads the audio already playing on your machine, so the video keeps running at normal speed and the captions trail slightly behind it, the same way broadcast captions do on television.

Accuracy tracks audio quality more than anything else. A host on a decent microphone in a quiet room transcribes cleanly. Heavy background music, several people talking over each other, or a low-bitrate mobile stream will produce more errors — the same conditions that degrade every captioning system, including the platforms' own.

Two practical adjustments help more than any setting: raise the stream volume a little relative to other system sounds, and set the source language explicitly rather than relying on auto-detection when you already know what language the stream is in.

If subtitles don't appear: four things to check

Almost every "no captions" report on a live stream traces back to one of the same four causes, and all of them are quick to rule out.

  • The audio source is set to Microphone. Microphone mode captions you, not the stream. For anything playing on screen, the source must be System Audio.
  • The stream is routed to a different output device. If your browser is playing through a headset while the app is listening to the desktop speakers, there is nothing to transcribe. Match the output device to the one the app is capturing.
  • The overlay is behind a fullscreen window. Borderless fullscreen usually behaves, but exclusive fullscreen can cover the overlay. Press Ctrl+Shift+L to switch into Game Mode, or move the overlay to a second monitor.
  • The wrong source language is selected. A stream in Korean being decoded as English produces confident nonsense rather than an obvious failure. Set the source language manually when you know it.

One more worth knowing: a muted browser tab produces no system audio at all. Lowering the volume is fine; muting removes the signal the app needs.
